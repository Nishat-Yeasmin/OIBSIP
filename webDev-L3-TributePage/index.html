HER STORY

A Life Dedicated to Discovery

Marie Curie was born Maria Skłodowska in Warsaw, Poland, in 1867. At a time when educational opportunities for women were limited, she pursued her studies with determination and eventually moved to Paris to continue her education at the University of Paris, where she studied physics and mathematics.

Together with her husband, Pierre Curie, she investigated unusual rays emitted by uranium. Her research led to the discovery of two new elements, polonium and radium, and helped establish the scientific study of radioactivity. Her work introduced new possibilities for understanding matter and its properties.

In 1903, Marie Curie received the Nobel Prize in Physics alongside Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel for their work on radiation phenomena. In 1911, she received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry for her discoveries of radium and polonium and for her investigation of the properties of radium.

During the First World War, Curie helped develop the use of mobile X-ray units that could assist doctors in treating wounded soldiers. She continued her scientific work throughout her life and became an enduring example of dedication, scientific curiosity, and perseverance.

HER LEGACY

Why Her Work Still Matters

Marie Curie's research helped establish radioactivity as an important field of scientific study. Her discoveries contributed to developments in physics, chemistry, and medical applications involving radiation. Beyond her scientific achievements, her career became an important example of persistence in scientific research and education.
